Why RAG breaks on engineering documentation — and five fixes

10 September 2026 · 14 min read

Almost every industrial RAG project I have seen fails in the same place, and it is not the place people expect. It is not the model. It is not the prompt. It is the document layer — and the failure is usually visible in the first week if you know what to look for.

The demo works. Somebody drops fifty PDFs into a vector store over a weekend, asks three questions, gets three good answers, and the project gets funded. Then the corpus grows to forty thousand documents, real engineers start asking real questions, and the answers quietly become wrong in ways that are hard to notice and expensive to trust.

Here is what is actually going on, and the five things that fixed it for us.

What makes engineering documentation different

General-purpose RAG is tuned for prose. Support articles, wiki pages, policy documents — text that flows left to right, top to bottom, where meaning survives being flattened into a string. Engineering documentation is not that. A typical industrial corpus contains:

  • Tables where the structure is the meaning. A clearance tolerance table is a grid of values indexed by frame size and operating condition. Flattened to text, the row and column headers detach from the numbers and the entire thing becomes noise that looks like signal.
  • Scanned documents from before digital typesetting. A 1987 manual is an image. Whatever OCR gives you is what the model sees, including the places where it confidently misread a 6 as an 8.
  • Units and tolerances that carry the answer. "0.15" means nothing. "0.15 mm ± 0.02" means something. "0.15 in" means something dangerously different.
  • Part numbers, equipment codes and revision identifiers that are lexically precise and semantically meaningless. Embeddings are very bad at these.
  • Multiple revisions of the same document, where the superseded version is often the one that indexes better because it is longer and cleaner.
  • Diagrams — P&IDs, cross-sections, wiring schematics — where the information is spatial and the surrounding text is a caption at best.

Every one of those breaks a default pipeline, and none of them show up in a fifty-document demo.

The diagnostic question. Ask your system something whose answer lives in a table, using a part number, from a document that has three revisions. If it answers confidently and correctly, you have a real system. If it answers confidently, you have a demo.

Fix 1 — Parse for structure, not for text

The single highest-return change we made was to stop treating PDF ingestion as text extraction and start treating it as structure extraction. Instead of a string per page, the parser emits a tree: sections with their heading path, tables as tables, figures with their captions and page references. 

{
  "doc_id": "manual-4471",
  "revision": "C",
  "path": ["Section 4", "4.3 Rotor Assembly", "4.3.2 Clearances"],
  "type": "table",
  "caption": "Table 4-7: Radial clearance by frame size",
  "columns": ["Frame size", "Nominal (mm)", "Min (mm)", "Max (mm)"],
  "rows": [
    ["A-200", "0.15", "0.13", "0.17"],
    ["A-300", "0.22", "0.19", "0.25"]
  ],
  "page": 87
}

That object then gets serialised into a chunk that keeps the heading path and the column headers attached to every row, so a retrieved fragment is self-describing. The model never sees a naked "0.15".

This is unglamorous work. Table detection on scanned pages is genuinely hard and no library solves it completely. But the return is larger than anything you will get from swapping models.

Fix 2 — Hybrid retrieval, because embeddings cannot spell

Dense vector search is excellent at "what is the procedure for cold-start commissioning" and useless at "what does fault code E-4471-B mean". Part numbers, error codes and equipment identifiers have almost no semantic content — two codes differing by one character are neighbours in embedding space and completely different in reality.

The fix is not exotic: run lexical retrieval alongside vector retrieval and fuse the results. Reciprocal rank fusion is enough, and it is about fifteen lines of code. 

def reciprocal_rank_fusion(rankings, k=60):
    """rankings: list of ranked doc_id lists, best first."""
    scores = {}
    for ranking in rankings:
        for rank, doc_id in enumerate(ranking):
            scores[doc_id] = scores.get(doc_id, 0) + 1.0 / (k + rank + 1)
    return sorted(scores, key=scores.get, reverse=True)


dense  = vector_index.search(query, top_k=50)
sparse = bm25_index.search(query, top_k=50)
candidates = reciprocal_rank_fusion([dense, sparse])[:20]
final = cross_encoder.rerank(query, candidates)[:5]

Add a cross-encoder re-ranker over the fused candidate set. Retrieval quality improves more from re-ranking twenty candidates than from doubling your embedding dimension.

Fix 3 — Normalise units at ingestion, not at generation

Do not ask the model to reason about units. It will, and it will occasionally be wrong, and you will not notice because the answer looks right.

Instead, extract and normalise measurements during ingestion. Every numeric quantity becomes a structured value with a magnitude, a unit and, where present, a tolerance. Store both the canonical SI form and the original as written, and always render the original in the answer so it matches the document the engineer will open to verify. 

"0.15 mm ± 0.02"  →  {
    "raw": "0.15 mm ± 0.02",
    "value": 0.15, "unit": "mm",
    "tolerance": {"plus": 0.02, "minus": 0.02},
    "si_value": 0.00015, "si_unit": "m"
}

This also gives you something valuable for free: a validator. If a generated answer contains a numeric quantity that does not appear in any retrieved chunk, that is a hallucination you can detect mechanically, before the user sees it.

Fix 4 — Make revisions and metadata first-class

In a corpus with revision history, the naive system will happily quote a superseded document. Nothing in the text says "this is out of date" — that information lives in metadata, and default pipelines discard metadata at the moment they create the chunk.

Three rules that fixed this for us:

  1. Every chunk carries document identity, revision, effective date and applicable equipment models as structured filter fields, not as text.
  2. Retrieval filters to the current revision by default. Superseded content is reachable only when the user explicitly asks for history.
  3. When the applicable equipment is known from context, filter on it. A question about an A-300 should never retrieve an A-200-only table, no matter how well it matches.

Filtering before ranking is dramatically cheaper than trying to teach a re-ranker about revision semantics, and it is auditable.

Fix 5 — Enforce citations, and let the system refuse

This is the change that had the biggest effect on adoption, and it is a product decision more than a technical one.

Every answer must cite the document and section it came from. Not as a footnote for politeness — as a hard requirement, checked after generation. If the answer contains a claim that cannot be traced to a retrieved chunk, the answer does not ship. The system says it could not find the information.

An assistant that says "I don't know" 15% of the time gets used. An assistant that is confidently wrong 2% of the time gets abandoned, because nobody can tell which 2%.

Engineers are extremely good at detecting a system that bluffs, and exactly one bad experience is enough to lose them. Refusal is not a degraded outcome. It is the feature that makes the rest of it usable.

What we measured

We built a golden set of a few hundred real questions with verified answers and source references, then gated every release on it. The metrics that mattered:

MetricWhat it asks
Retrieval recall@kIs the correct source chunk in the candidate set at all? If not, nothing downstream can save you.
GroundednessIs every claim in the answer supported by a retrieved chunk?
Citation accuracyDoes the cited section actually contain the claim, or is the citation decorative?
Numeric fidelityDoes every number in the answer appear, with its unit, in a retrieved chunk?
Appropriate refusalWhen the answer is genuinely not in the corpus, does the system say so?

Note what is absent: no helpfulness score, no preference rating, no LLM-as-judge on overall quality. Those are useful for chatbots. They are the wrong instrument when the output is a clearance tolerance.

Across three release cycles, ungrounded answers fell by roughly 40%. Not one percent of that came from a better base model. It came from parsing, retrieval, filtering and refusal.

The order I would do it in again

  1. Build the golden set first, before any pipeline work. You cannot improve what you cannot measure, and building it forces you to talk to the engineers who will use the system.
  2. Fix parsing and chunking. Biggest single lever, least interesting work.
  3. Add hybrid retrieval and a re-ranker.
  4. Add metadata filtering, especially revisions.
  5. Add citation enforcement and refusal.
  6. Only now consider a bigger model. You will usually find you do not need one.

The uncomfortable summary. Roughly 80% of the work in a production industrial RAG system is document engineering, and roughly 5% is anything to do with the language model. The industry's attention is allocated in almost exactly the inverse proportion.
